Research, Explained

Plain-language tours of my major work in quantum & nonlinear photonics

My research turns abstract quantum and nonlinear optics into working chips. Below I unpack a few of my favorite results — what we built, why it was hard, and why it matters — without assuming a physics background. For the full record, see my CV or Google Scholar.

Nature · 2026 · Co-first author

An optical amplifier small and efficient enough to live on a chip

Dean†, Park†, et al., "Low-power integrated optical amplification through second-harmonic resonance," Nature 649, 1159 (2026).  † co-first author

Almost every optical system eventually needs to make light stronger — to push a signal down a fiber, read out a faint sensor, or drive the next stage of a circuit. On a chip, that has always been the hard part. Compact optical amplifiers are notoriously power-hungry, and the efficient ones are bulky lab instruments.

We built an amplifier that breaks that trade-off. Instead of brute-forcing the gain, our device recycles energy through a resonant design: it traps the "pump" light and its second harmonic in the same tiny structure so the same photons do useful work over and over. The payoff is dramatic — the chip intensifies light roughly 100× (>17 dB of gain) on only a few hundred milliwatts of power, an order-of-magnitude leap over previous integrated amplifiers, while adding very little noise and working across a broad slice of the spectrum.

"Low power" is what makes it matter. An amplifier this efficient could run on a battery, which opens the door to optical technology inside everyday devices — biosensors, portable diagnostics, data-communication links, and compact light sources — rather than only on an optical table.

Why it mattersA foundational building block — efficient gain — finally made small and low-power enough to integrate, unlocking portable photonic systems.

Science Advances · 2024 · First author

Squeezing the quantum noise of light — on a single chip

Park, et al., "Single-mode squeezed-light generation and tomography with an integrated optical parametric oscillator," Science Advances 10(11), eadl1814 (2024).

Even a "perfect" laser carries an irreducible fuzziness called quantum noise — the fundamental flicker that sets the floor on how precisely we can measure with light. "Squeezed light" is a special quantum state that pushes that noise below the standard limit in one variable (by paying the price in another), and it's the secret ingredient behind record-breaking sensors, including the gravitational-wave detectors at LIGO.

The catch: squeezed light has traditionally needed an entire optical table of mirrors and crystals. In this work I demonstrated a squeezed-light source built entirely on a thin-film lithium niobate chip, using an on-chip optical parametric oscillator. Critically, I also performed full quantum-state tomography on-platform — reconstructing the complete quantum portrait of the light to prove it was genuinely squeezed, not just quiet by accident.

This is the kind of result that connects two worlds: the fragile, table-sized physics of quantum optics and the manufacturable, scalable reality of integrated photonics. A chip-scale squeezer is a building block for quantum sensing, quantum-enhanced measurement, and continuous-variable quantum information.

Why it mattersOne of the first on-chip squeezed-light sources with full tomographic verification on this platform — bringing a key quantum resource onto a scalable, fabricated chip.
Optics Letters · 2022 · First author

Making bright blue light efficiently on a chip

Park, et al., "High-efficiency second harmonic generation of blue light on thin-film lithium niobate," Optics Letters 47(11), 2706 (2022).

Short-wavelength visible light — blue in particular — is in constant demand: for quantum computers that control trapped atoms, for high-density data storage, and for biology and displays. One powerful way to make it is second-harmonic generation, in which infrared light is sent into a nonlinear material that fuses pairs of photons into single photons of double the energy (and half the wavelength), converting invisible infrared into bright visible light.

Doing this efficiently at short wavelengths on a chip is genuinely hard — the materials are fussy and the engineering tolerances are tight. By carefully designing and poling thin-film lithium niobate waveguides, I demonstrated blue-light generation at 456 nm with a normalized conversion efficiency of ~33,000% / W·cm², among the highest reported on this platform at the time.

Beyond the headline number, this work built up fabrication and design know-how — how to make these tiny structures actually perform at short, demanding wavelengths.

Why it mattersEfficient on-chip blue light is a key enabler for atom-based quantum computing, sensing, and compact visible light sources.
